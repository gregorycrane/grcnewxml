PREFACE
THE main part of the following pages, the commentary, was
written as a thesis for the degree of Bachelor of Letters; its
purpose was to throw some light on the history of Vespasian's
reign-a period badly documented, and therefore neglected by
modern writers-by means of an historical commentary on
Suetonius' Life; for this work, whatever its shortcomings, is
our chief literary source for the reign, and the one recom-
mended, faute de mieux, to students in the School of Literae
Humaniores at Oxford. The commentary is here printed
with a few additions and a short introduction dealing with the
general method and historical value of the Life. The text
and apparatus are those of Ihm's editio minor, reprinted by
kind permission of B. G. Teubner, of Leipzig.
My grateful acknowledgement of assistance and criticism is
due to Mr. H. M. Last, of St. John's College, who, moreover,
first suggested this subject to me; to Mr. P. V. M. Benecke,
of Magdalen College, and Mr. G. H. Stevenson, of University
College, the examiners of my thesis; and, above all, to
Professor Stuart Jones, who has given me invaluable help at
every stage of the book's progress, endeavouring, in Suetonius'
words, stabilire primo, deinde et ornare.
A. W. B.

I. SUETONIUS
C. SUETONIUS Tranquillus, the son of an eques, Suetonius
Laetus, tribune of the XIII Gemina legion,¹ was born, probably
at Rome, in or about the year A. D. 69. This date has been
established by Macé; the date 77 was previously suggested
by Mommsen, on the grounds that Suetonius had thoughts of
accepting a military tribunate in 101, and that this office was
assumed in the senatorial cursus at the age of twenty-five. But
Mommsen himself admitted that there was no evidence for
assuming the same age-limit in the equestrian cursus, and
Macé, after emphasizing this point, shows clearly that Momm-
sen's date would make Suetonius too young, firstly, to be
treated by Pliny as an equal (contubernalis), and secondly,
to receive the ius trium liberorum in 112 or 1136 from an
emperor peculiarly loath to grant this privilege. Macé's own
date rests on the fact that Suetonius describes himself as an
adulescens in 88; this means, in his usage, that he was then
between eighteen and twenty-eight, and could not have been
born later than 70; on the other hand, it is unlikely that
Suetonius Laetus would have married before the return of the
XIII legion to Rome in 68, in the last days of Nero, and we
may thus fix the year 69 as the approximate date of Sueto-
nius' birth.

IV. GENERAL CHARACTERISTICS OF THE 'LIFE
OF VESPASIAN'
Suetonius' de Vita Caesarum is a work of great significance
in the history of Latin literature. For it inaugurated a new
method of historical writing which was to supersede almost
entirely the 'annalistic' method of the past: after him in Latin
literature there is only one 'annalistic' historian, Ammianus
Marcellinus; all the rest follow the Suetonian method. That
this change was for the good of Latin literature or history can
scarcely be maintained, but at any rate Suetonius deserves the
credit of introducing and standardizing an immensely popular
historical form.
The origin of that method and the cause of its invasion
into the realm of history are easy to trace. With the establish-
ment and ever-increasing power of the principate, Roman
historical writing had come more and more, necessarily, to deal
with the actions and personality of one man, the emperor, and
to treat the events of his reign primarily in their relationship
to him. Most of the historians of the first century of the
principate, Aufidius Bassus, Pliny, and the rest, are lost, but
we can see to what a degree the emperor has monopolized

are on a higher plane historically than others. It has been
shown in the preceding section that Suetonius for the first part
of the Life closely followed historical works previously pub-
lished, but for the second part collected his own material and
co-ordinated it for himself. We may expect, then, to detect
a difference in method where the one part ends and the other
begins. And this difference is certainly considerable. The
first seven chapters of the Life form a connected story, relating
the events of Vespasian's life in chronological order, with some
emphasizing of unimportant points, it is true,¹ but on the whole
continuous and lucid. Suetonius' source was not always accu-
rate: the short account of the Jewish War of 66-8 has four
discrepancies on points of detail with the narrative of the
eye-witness Josephus. Moreover, there is one astonishing
omission. Suetonius not only says nothing of the great revolt
on the Rhine in A.D. 70, which must have had its place in
Pliny's history, but seems quite ignorant of the fact that it
was largely responsible for the changes he relates as made by
Vespasian in the army. But in the main this part of the Life
is a good piece of historical writing, and an example of Sueto-
nius' power of reproducing a literary source in a small compass
without losing the interest of the story.

parsimony, his love of broad humour. As a history of Ves-
pasian's reign the Life is entirely inadequate. We find
references, more or less casual and incomplete, to Vespasian's
reorganization of the army and of the eastern frontier, his
work in restoring the national finances, his tenure of the censor-
ship, and his relations with the Senate and its intransigent
members; but there is no mention at all of many events which
we regard as all-important, such as the annexation of the Agri
Decumates, the granting of ius Latii to Spain, and the new
constitutional position given to the Emperor's heir. Instead we
are given a selection of anecdotes and bons mots to exemplify
Vespasian's meanness and good humour.
Suetonius, of course, as we have seen, was not really an his-
torian, nor a systematic biographer, but a disciple of the
grammarians; it is only when he is closely following some
professed historian that he comes to write history, almost
against his will; left to himself, it is the emperor and not the
reign that interests him. And in his own line he has many
admirable qualities. The picture of Vespasian that we can
read between the lines is most vivid: we are left with the
impression of a very human old man, clearly not bred to the
purple, and with some of the vices of his origin-coarse, that is
to say, and in money matters ready to exact the last penny
due, but dignified, efficient, and generous. The charges
levelled against him of meanness and extortion are carefully
examined, and Suetonius is able to come to a considered and
just conclusion about them. Finally, we must credit Suetonius
with some appreciation at least of Vespasian's purpose and
policy: a sentence inserted apparently at haphazard into the
Life is an exact description of the significance of the reign in
Roman history-(Vespasian) per totum imperii tempus nihil
habuit antiquius quam prope afflictam nutantemque rem publicam
stabilire primo, deinde et ornare.

NOTE ON THE MANUSCRIPTS
All our extant MSS. of the de Vita Caesarum derive from
one archetype, and this seems to have been a MS. that existed
at Fulda in Germany in the middle of the 9th century; of
this a copy was sent, about that time, to Servatus Lupus,
abbot of Ferrara, and our oldest and best MS., the codex
Memmianus, now at Paris, dating also from the 9th century,
will be closely akin to this copy. From this time onward the
MS. was extensively copied, and the versions made suffer
more and more from error and interpolation. Apart from the
codex Memmianus, Ihm makes most use of the codex Gudianus
(11th century) and the codex Vaticanus (11th-12th century);
this last, though later, is better than the codex Gudianus, but
stops at chapter 3 of the Life of Caligula. Other MSS.
used by Ihm are divided by him into two classes, one keeping
closely to the readings of the archetype, the other containing
many interpolations and variations. An early (9th century)
collection of extracts from the Lives is also used by Ihm.
The researches of Ihm and Preud'homme, showing that our
extant MSS. are of a common origin, have now placed the text
of the de Vita Caesarum on a firm basis. Some scholars still
hold that certain MSS. of the 15th century, giving plausible
variant readings, have independent authority, but until this has
been demonstrated more clearly, it seems better to accept the
explanation of Ihm and Preud'homme that these readings are
due to the conjectures of scribes and may be ignored in the
reconstruction of the original text,

CHAPTER I
THE eighth book of Suetonius' Lives of the Caesars contains his
account of the three Flavian emperors, and this first chapter is
a general introduction to the whole, giving (1) an estimate of the
dynasty, (2) an account of its ancestry. The life of Vespasian
proper begins with c. 2 Vespasianus natus est; cf. Titus...natus
est (Tit. 1), Domitianus natus est (Dom. 1).
§ 1. sine ullis maiorum imaginibus: used of a man none of
whose ancestors have held curule office, i. e. have attained the rank
of aedile. See Cicero, Verrines, 5. 14. 36.
§ 2. T. Flavius Petro. His wife's name was Tertulla (c. 2).
We know no more of him than Suetonius tells us, but he must have
been a very old man at the time of Vespasian's birth, if alive at all.

We may note, with regard to the succession, that Vespasian, in
spite of his confidence, left nothing to chance. From the very
beginning of the reign everything was done to make the accession
of Titus, and of Domitian after him, as certain and smooth as
possible. Titus became Caesar and princeps iuventutis (titles
reserved for heirs of the imperial house) before the end of 69; later,
he was his father's colleague in the censorship and in seven eponymous
consulships (see on c. 8); he received the tribunicia potestas from
Ist July, 71 (when Vespasian became trib. pot. III); he was saluted
as imperator on the occasion of the Jewish triumph in 71 (see on
c. 8), and thenceforward his salutations and his father's are con-
current; &c. It is clear, however, that Titus was not regarded as
an equal sharer in his father's imperial powers: this point, though
not strictly relevant, may perhaps be briefly considered here.
There is literary evidence to suggest that during Titus' stay in
the East, there was friction between the father and the son with
regard to Titus' position in the empire (Suet. Tit. 5), and we know
that Titus allowed coins to be issued in the East giving him the
praenomen Imperatoris, and accepted the salutation as imperator
from the troops on the occasion of the fall of Jerusalem, though
technically he was only his father's legatus. It is this, no doubt,
that explains the passage of Philostratus, who says of Titus 'Αναρρη-
θεὶς δ᾽ αὐτοκράτωρ ἐν τῇ ῾Ῥώμῃ καὶ ἀριστείων ἀξιωθεὶς τούτων ἀπῄει μὲν
ἰσομοιρήσων τῆς ἀρχῆς τῷ πατρί (Apoll. 6. 30). But on Titus' return
to Rome, we see clearly that the praenomen Imperatoris was
definitely withheld from him, though in one coin-issue of 71 he is
apparently described as designatus Imperator. His customary
official title on the senatorial bronze is T. Caesar Vespasianus Imp.
(with or without the number of salutations), and on the gold and
silver T. Caesar Imp. Vespasianus: in either case the title
Imperator is used as a cognomen only. (The use of the praenomen
